# Pitch interrogation — the twelve questions

Contents: [How to use](#how-to-use-this) · [The twelve](#the-twelve) · [The three that kill most ideas](#the-three-that-kill-most-ideas) · [Scoring the answers](#scoring-the-answers)

## How to use this

A ranked table says *which* gap. It does not say whether you can **defend** the gap out loud, to a partner who has heard four hundred versions of it this month. Run these twelve against every candidate you are seriously recommending — and against the top three at minimum, because comparing answers side by side exposes which idea is actually alive.

**Answer in the founder's voice, not the analyst's.** Short declarative sentences. No "leverages," no "revolutionises," no "AI-powered platform." If an answer needs a paragraph of setup before it makes sense, that is the finding.

**An unanswerable question is a result, not a gap in the research.** Write "no answer" and let it stand. Three "no answers" in this list means the candidate is not ready, regardless of its score.

## The twelve

### 1. The one-liner — under 50 characters
What it is, flatly. A noun phrase a stranger can repeat.

*Good:* "Agentic video editor for professionals." *Bad:* "AI-powered creative platform to revolutionise content creation."
The test: could a partner repeat it to a colleague an hour later without looking at notes?

### 2. Why isn't this just a feature of `<nearest incumbent>`?
Name the incumbent by name. Then give the **mechanism** — the `Barred` answer from the scan, said in one sentence a non-expert understands.

*Good:* "The party with the data is the party that caused the problem; their legal team will never let them publish it."
*Bad:* "We're more focused / faster / more specialised." Those are not mechanisms, those are hopes.
If the honest answer is execution rather than structure, **say that** — "they can build it, they won't build it well, and here is why we accumulate faster than they can catch up" — and know it is a harder argument that needs evidence behind it.

### 3. What exists today, and what specifically changes?
Two columns. **Today:** the actual workaround people use right now — a spreadsheet, a Slack channel, a consultant, a cron job, nothing at all. **After:** what is different on a Tuesday afternoon for one named person.

If "today" is "nothing, nobody does this," that is usually a demand problem rather than an opportunity. People with real pain have *always* built a bad workaround. **Find the bad workaround.** Its existence is your best evidence, and its shape tells you what to build.

### 4. Why now? What became true in the last 18 months?
There must be a **change**, not just a **trend**. A capability that crossed a threshold, a cost that collapsed, a regulation that landed, a behaviour that went mainstream, a platform that opened.

*Good:* "This was impossible before X, which shipped in March, and the first documented incident was in February."
*Bad:* "AI is growing fast." That was true three years ago and will be true in three years, so it explains nothing about today.
The follow-up a partner will ask: **"why didn't this exist two years ago, and why won't it be obvious to everyone in six months?"** Have both halves.

### 5. Who pays the first $10, by name?
Not a segment. A name, or a description so specific it is almost a name — "the platform engineer at a 200-person company who already pays for three developer tools out of a discretionary budget."

Then: **how do you reach that person this week?** If the answer is "content marketing" or "we'll launch on Product Hunt," you do not have a first customer, you have a hope.

### 6. Is the user the buyer?
Very often not. Name both populations separately.
- **User:** who touches it daily
- **Buyer:** who signs
- **Champion:** who argues for it internally

When they differ, the distance between them is the go-to-market problem, and pretending they are one person is how a pitch quietly dies in diligence. If the buyer does not exist yet as a category — for instance, you are selling to a compliance function nobody has staffed — say so. That is honest and sometimes it is the whole timing thesis.

### 7. The world-after test — what happens if everyone adopts this?
Describe the world where this won. Two things to surface:
- **Does anything meaningfully improve**, or has the work merely moved sideways?
- **Does it get harder to displace you as adoption grows?** Network effects, accumulated corpus, integration depth, switching cost. If adoption makes you *more* replaceable, you built a commodity.

A good answer implies a second and third product without naming them. A weak answer describes a slightly tidier version of today.

### 8. Bottom-up path to $100M
Never top-down. Never "the market is $50B and we take 2%" — a partner has heard that from everyone and it signals you have not done the arithmetic.

**N customers × $P × expansion**, with N and P defended. *"4,000 teams at $2K/month"* invites the real question — *are there 4,000 such teams, and why would the 4,000th buy?* — which is the question worth being able to answer.

### 9. Why you two?
Not credentials. **Evidence.** What have you already shipped on this exact surface, who used it, and what did you learn that someone starting today does not know?

Both founders get an answer. The partner will probe both, and an unstated co-founder story reads as a weak co-founder.

### 10. The pre-mortem — what kills this?
It is eighteen months out and the company is dead. **Why?** Name the single most likely cause, honestly.

Answering "nothing, we've thought of everything" is the worst available answer. A founder who names their own throat convincingly is more fundable than one who has not looked. Then: what is the earliest cheap signal that this is happening, and what do you do when you see it?

### 11. Future resonance — three years out, more needed or less?
Does the underlying problem **grow** with the trend everyone agrees on, or does it get solved by it?

The trap: gaps caused by immaturity close on their own as the ecosystem matures, and you are then competing with the tide. Gaps caused by **structural conflict** persist regardless of maturity — and gaps that *scale with adoption* are best of all, because every new participant makes the problem worse and your position better.
Ask: *does the thing that makes this painful get bigger or smaller as the category grows?*

### 12. Wedge versus vision — and why this wedge first
One sentence of vision. One sentence of wedge. The connection between them must be **mechanical**, not aspirational: the wedge must produce an asset — data, distribution, integration position, trust — that makes step two cheaper for you than for anyone else.

*Good:* "We start here because it is the only place we can get the corpus, and the corpus is what makes the rest possible."
*Bad:* "We start here and then expand into adjacent verticals." That is a hope with a diagram.

## The three that kill most ideas

If you only have time for three, use **#2** (why not a feature), **#5** (who pays first, by name), and **#10** (what kills this). Between them they catch the three commonest failures: no mechanism, no reachable customer, and no self-awareness.

## Scoring the answers

Mark each answer **Strong / Weak / No answer**.

- **3+ "no answer"** → not ready to pitch. Do the work, do not write the application.
- **#2 or #5 weak** → the wedge is wrong even if the market is right. Re-run the scan.
- **#4 weak but everything else strong** → the idea is fine and the *timing story* is missing. That is a writing problem, and it is fixable in an afternoon.
- **All strong but #7 flat** → a good business that is not a venture business. Worth saying plainly rather than dressing up.
